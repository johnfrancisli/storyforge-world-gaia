At the headland's lee, where a natural shelf of basalt breaks the wind and the spray, the Skin-Boat Sheds are Hvalhöfn's harbor—a row of low, driftwood-framed shelters, each roofed with oiled hides and each holding a single skin-boat, the whalers' fragile, deadly craft that are to Hvalhöfn what longships are to Skaldvik. The boats are built over whalebone frames, their skins of sewn seal-hide stretched tight and oiled to a dark, supple finish that gleams when wet, and they are light enough for two men to carry but strong enough to ride the ice-floe swells where a timber boat would be stove in. Each shed is marked with its boat's name—Whale-Bane, Ice-Biter, Cold-Sister—and a carved figurehead on the shed's lintel that the whalers touch for luck before every putting-out. The sheds smell of seal-oil, wet hide, and the faint, sharp scent of the pine-pitch used to seal the seams, and the boat-builders—a small, specialized crew who work only in the dark months when the boats are ashore—can re-skin a boat in three days and would stake their oil-shares on every stitch. The skin-boats are Hvalhöfn's lifeline, and a boat-builder is a more important man than a whaling-captain, for without the boats there is no hunt.
