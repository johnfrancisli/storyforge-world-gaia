Motu Lotu is the refuge-island of the Tide Archipelago, a low sandy motu on the archipelago's windward edge that has become the gathering-place for the islanders displaced by the rising seas that are slowly drowning the outer atolls — families whose home-islands have been swallowed by the waves, fishermen whose reefs have gone dead, whole clans who arrived with nothing but the canoes they fled in. The island itself is unprepossessing — a crescent of white sand with a thin fringe of wind-bent palms and a single brackish well — but it has been built up with a sprawling camp of shelters of driftwood, palm-thatch, and salvaged canoe-sail, their walls patched with bark-cloth and matting, stretching along the crescent beach and inland among the palms in a dense, improvised warren of lanes and compounds that grows every season as more boats arrive. The sound of Motu Lotu is unlike any other island — not the settled hum of a village but a shifting chorus of many island dialects, the crying of infants, the low voices of elders telling the stories of drowned homes, and the constant work of building, patching, and preparing. The smell is of salt, damp thatch, cook-smoke, and the faint undertone of grief that no wind fully carries away. The people here are hospitable to a fault — they have known loss — and they share what little they have, but the question that hangs over every fire is the same: where will we go when this island too goes under.
