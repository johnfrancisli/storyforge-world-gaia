The Student Dormitory is the living-quarters of Hokuala, a long low building of palm-thatch and woven-reed walls on the lagoon-shore near the school, divided into sleeping-bays of woven mats on a raised sand floor where the students — some forty children and youths at any time — sleep, eat, and keep their few possessions. The bays are communal, eight to a side, each with a small carved cubby for personal things and a hook for the wrap that is each student's only garment, and the air is close and warm with the smell of young bodies, salt, palm-fibre mats, and the green tea that is always brewing on the dormitory's small cook-hearth. The students rise before dawn for the first star-watch, train through the morning on the lagoon, attend the school in the heat of the day, and return to the star-stones or the lagoon at dusk, so the dormitory is busiest at midday rest and in the brief hours of sleep, when the chant of the star-path songs can be heard muttered by students drilling in their mats even in their dreams. A dormitory-elder, a senior student in the last year of training, keeps order and rouses the sleepers, and the walls are hung with the students' practice star-charts, drawn and redrawn in charcoal on bark-paper, the evidence of years of learning.
