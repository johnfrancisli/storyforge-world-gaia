The Mosque of the Tides is the chief mosque of Basur, built on a rocky point at the harbour mouth where the breakwater meets the shore, its walls washed white and its single dome green-tiled, reflected in the water so that at high tide it seems to float on the sea. It is dedicated to the sea-djinn and the spirit of the strait, and the sailors and fishermen of Basur come here before every voyage to pray for safe passage and to make offerings — a coin dropped into the tidal pool beneath the mosque, a length of cloth tied to the railing, a candle lit at the sea-shrine. The prayer-hall is simple and bright, its floor of worn stone, its windows open to the sea-breeze, and its qibla wall holding a great mirror of polished brass said to reflect the true heart of any who pray before it. The imam, a one-armed old sailor turned scholar, preaches the binding-law as it applies to the sea — the contracts of ship and crew and cargo, the obligations to the sea-djinn, the oaths that bind a captain to his ship. At the spring and autumn equinoxes a great ceremony is held here to renew the city's pact with the strait, and the whole fleet puts in to attend.
