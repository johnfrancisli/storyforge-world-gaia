The Serpent's Crown is the summit shrine of the great pyramid, reached by climbing the central stairway up nine terraces, each carved with coiled serpents whose stone heads have been worn smooth by centuries of rain and hands. The summit is a small flat court with a single shrine-room open to the sky, its walls carved with a great feathered serpent biting its own tail and its floor cracked by the roots of a strangler fig that grows through the roof and spreads its canopy overhead. The loremasters have cleared the worst of the jungle but leave the fig, believing it a pact between the old builders and the forest. Offerings of copal, flowers, and maize-bread are left on a stone altar each morning by the shrine-keeper, and the smoke drifts up through the fig-branches into the sky. From the summit you can see the whole ruined city spread below — the plazas, the half-cleared temples, the new thatched houses on old foundations — and beyond it the unbroken green sea of the jungle stretching to every horizon, the roar of howler monkeys rising from it like the voice of the forest itself.
