The Netmaker's Shed is the craft-house of Makapaa, a long low building of stacked limestone and driftwood on the widest ledge of the village, open on three sides to the wind so that the net-makers can see their work by the grey sea-light and the hanging nets can dry in the salt breeze. Here the women and old men of the island spin cordage from coconut fibre and hibiscus bark on drop-spindles, their hands moving in a rhythm as old as the cliffs, and knot the great fishing-nets — seine nets, gill-nets, and the deep-water hand-lines weighted with stone sinkers — on frames of driftwood strung between the shed's posts. The work is constant: nets tear on the rocks and must be mended, new nets are always needed, and the cordage is traded to the other islands for taro and cloth. The shed is the social heart of the village, and the net-makers sing as they work — wayfinding fragments and fishing-chants that carry over the wind, their voices thin against the gull-cries and the sea. The floor is littered with fibre-stuff and the air smells of dry coconut husk, salt, and the faint fish-oil of the used nets hung to mend. The net-mistress, an old woman with hands gnarled from a lifetime of knotting, keeps the tallies and the trade-cordage, and teaches the girls the knot-counts.
