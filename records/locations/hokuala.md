Hokuala is the wayfinder's island of the Tide Archipelago, a low green atoll of palm-fringed sand and a sheltered lagoon set aside for the training of navigators, the keepers of the star-paths and the song-magic that let the archipelago's voyaging canoes find their way across hundreds of leagues of open sea. The island is quieter than the others — no market, no great shrine, no politics — and its population is young, the students who come from every island to learn the wayfinder's art and the masters who teach them, walking the beaches at night with their eyes on the stars, their lips moving as they learn the star-path songs that encode the rising and setting points of a hundred stars and the seamarks between. By day the lagoon is busy with training-canoes — small outriggers sailed solo by students practicing the techniques of wayfinding: reading the swell-patterns, the wind-curls, the bird-flights, the phosphorescence, the cloud-signs of distant land — and the air carries the rhythmic chanting of the song-magic lessons, the masters' voices low and the students' voices rising in response, a sound like the sea itself learning to speak. The sand is marked with navigation circles drawn by the masters, diagrams of stones and sticks laid out on the beach to teach the star-compass, and the whole island feels like a great instrument tuned to the sky and the water. The air smells of salt, palm, and the green tea the wayfinders drink to keep alert through the night-watches.
