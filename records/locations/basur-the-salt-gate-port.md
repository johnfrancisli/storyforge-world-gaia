Basur is the great sea-port of Al-Khayzar, built where the desert meets the turquoise waters of the southern strait at a natural harbour walled by two long breakwaters of black volcanic rock that the old builders raised and the sea has not yet worn down. The city climbs the red-stone headland above the harbour in terraces of whitewashed lime, its houses flat-roofed and crowded with wind-catchers that turn in the sea-breeze and funnel cool air down into the rooms, and from the water it looks like a pile of sugar-cubes rising out of the sand. The harbour is dense with ships — great dhows with lateen sails patched in a hundred colours, merchant galleys from foreign ports, fishing boats, and the sleek cutter-galleys of the port-guards — and the quays are a continuous market of unloading: bales of spice from Zaffar, ingots of copper and iron from beyond the sea, dyed silks, amber, slaves, ivory, and the salt that gives the port its name, panned from the salt-flats north of the city in shallow pans that glitter white in the sun. The air is thick with salt, tar, fish, and the perfume of spices spilled from broken bales, and the muezzin's call competes with the shouts of stevedores, the creak of rigging, and the slap of water on stone. Basur is where Al-Khayzar meets the wider world, and its people are cosmopolitan and sharp — merchants, sailors, pirates-turned-traders, and the agents of foreign powers all rubbing shoulders in the coffee-houses.
