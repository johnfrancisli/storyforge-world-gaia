The black-sand shore below the headland, where the whalers drag their kills and the air is a thick, greasy fog of blood and rendered fat, the Flensing Strand is Hvalhöfn's works-floor—the place where the whale becomes the goods that keep the station alive. Great iron cauldrons sit over driftwood fires along the tide-line, their contents bubbling to clear oil that is skimmed into barrels and rolled up the headland by hand, and the beach itself is a slick, trampled field of blubber-scraps, bone-shards, and the dark, tarry residue of a thousand renderings. Flensing racks of whalebone and timber line the upper strand, strung with strips of meat and slabs of blubber that cure in the bitter wind, and the whalers work the carcasses with long, curved flensing-knives in a slow, skilled dance that strips a whale to the bone in a single tide. The gulls are a constant, screaming cloud above the strand, and the station's dogs—shaggy, yellow-eyed beasts bred to guard the meat from the birds and the bears—patrol the racks with a territorial snarl. The strand is the hardest, ugliest, most essential place in Hvalhöfn, and the men and women who work it are paid in oil-shares and the grim pride of a job that the nation cannot do without.
