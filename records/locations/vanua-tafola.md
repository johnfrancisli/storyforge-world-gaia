  law, a balance it guards jealously.

Vanua Tafola is the floating market-town of the Tide Archipelago, not an island at all but a vast raft-city of lashed bamboo, balsa, and cork-wood platforms moored in a broad sheltered lagoon between two low coral islets, its hundreds of structures rising and falling with the gentle swell and connected by plank-bridges and rope-walks that flex and creak with every shift of the water. The town is the great trade-hub of the archipelago — here the smoked fish of Makapaa, the obsidian of Motu Peleki, the breadfruit and taro of a dozen islands, the cordage and cloth and shell-work of the craft-villages, and the rare goods of foreign traders who come across the sea, all meet and change hands in a constant roar of haggling that carries across the water from dawn to dusk. The floats are colorfully painted — red, blue, yellow, the better to see them from a canoe — and hung with streamers and signal-flags, and the whole town rocks gently, so that a visitor's sea-legs never quite leave them. The smell is a shifting mosaic — salt, fish, ripe fruit, woodsmoke, dyed cloth, the tarry caulking of the floats, the heavy sweetness of fermented palm-wine — and the sound is of water, voices, the slap of hulls against the floats, and the music of a dozen buskers playing nose-flute and slit-drum for coin. At night the town is lit by clay oil-lamps that turn the lagoon into a field of trembling gold, and the trade goes on by lamplight, quieter but unending.
