The Black-Glass Forge is the great workshop of Motu Peleki, built into a natural lava-tube on the island's mid-slope where geothermal heat rising from below keeps the stone walls hot to the touch year-round. The smiths of Peleki — muscular, soot-streaked men and women with ritual burn-scars on their forearms — work obsidian here, fracturing the glassy stone into knife-blades, spear-points, and the ceremonial adzes that are the island's chief export and prestige good. The work is done by percussion and pressure-flaking on anvils of basalt, the crack and ring of stone echoing through the tube, and the finished blades are sharper than any steel and hold an edge that the archipelago's chiefs prize above metal. A vent deeper in the tube keeps a permanent low fire burning without fuel, used to heat-treat the obsidian and to work the small amounts of native copper that bubble up in the lava-flows. The air is hot, dry, and sharp with the mineral smell of hot stone and the acrid tang of sulphur from the deeper vent. Apprentices serve a harsh seven-year bond, and the master smith, a one-eyed woman called the Glass-Mother, tests each finished blade on her own scarred arm.
