The Anchorage is the harbour of Tidal Reach, the sheltered water between the old stone pillars at the river-mouth where the fishing fleet and the larger trading dhows lie at anchor or tied to mooring-posts driven into the mud. At dawn the fleet puts out to sea, a hundred outrigger canoes and a dozen dhows slipping past the pillars with the tide, their blue-and-white sails catching the first wind, and at dusk they return in a long straggling line heavy with fish, the water alive with gulls. The anchorage is always busy — boats being caulked and tarred on the mudflats at low tide, nets mended on the walkways, sails dried on lines strung between the stilt-houses, and pilots shouting directions to incoming river-barges. A boatyard on the northern shore builds and repairs the outriggers, its beach strewn with hulls in every state of construction, and the air smells of wood-shavings, hot tar, hemp, and salt. The harbour-master's post sits on a high stilt-platform at the mouth, from which she watches all traffic and flies signal-flags to guide vessels in over the shifting sandbars.
