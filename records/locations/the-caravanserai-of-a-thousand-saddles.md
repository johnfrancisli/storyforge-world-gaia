The Caravanserai of a Thousand Saddles is the great staging-inn of Zaffar, a vast square fortress of mud-brick and lime at the city's desert gate, its walls enclosing a single enormous courtyard large enough to hold three hundred camels and their loads, surrounded on all four sides by arcades of stables on the ground floor and lodgings on the upper. The courtyard is a sea of camels at all hours — kneeling, groaning, being loaded and unloaded, their drivers shouting and cursing — and the arcades are hung with saddles, saddlebags, rope, and the banners of the caravan-clans, so that the place takes its name from the thousand saddles that dangle above the yard like a forest of leather. The smell is of camel, leather, dung-smoke, and the coffee that is always brewing in the great copper pots of the caravanserai's kitchen, served in tiny cups to anyone with coin. The innkeeper, a fat and jovial man who knows every road and every clan, keeps the register of caravans and matches drivers to loads, and his word settles the disputes that flare over watering-order and stall-space. A mosque-chapel sits in one corner for the travellers' prayers, and a small bath-house offers steam and water to the road-weary.
