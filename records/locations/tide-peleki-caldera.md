Peleki's Caldera is the molten heart of Motu Peleki, a vast circular crater at the island's summit where a lake of churning lava — bright as the sun at its core, crusting to dull black at its edges — heaves and spits in slow, relentless motion. The heat is staggering at the rim, a wall of shimmering air that bends the view of the sea below into rippling waves, and the ground is too hot to barefoot; the Fire-Voices approach along a paved path of black stone laid by their ancestors. Offerings are cast into the lake at every new moon — breadfruit, woven leis, and the carved obsidian figures the islanders call fire-children — each consumed in a hissing flare as it meets the rock. The three Fire-Voices chant the old placation-songs here in a tongue no other islander fully understands, their voices rising and falling with the lava's surge, and the Caldera-Mother alone stands at the very lip, reading Peleki's will in the pattern of the fountains and the colour of the crust. The air tastes of metal and sulphur, the wind carries fine ash that stings the eyes, and the lowboom of the mountain's breath shakes the chest. No one comes here except the Fire-Voices and those they summon for judgment or blessing.
