The Sea-Cave Shrine is the spirit-lodge of Makapaa, set in a small lateral cave off the main harbor-cave, reached by a low crawl through a gap that opens into a hidden chamber where the roof is pierced by a natural shaft and sunlight falls in a single bright column onto a tidal pool that fills and empties with the sea through a crack in the floor. The shrine is the pool itself and the stone around it, carved with the coiled forms of octopus and the staring faces of sea-spirits by hands older than the village, and the cave-walls are hung with offerings — fish-bones strung on cord, carved driftwood charms, and the dried first-catch of every season returned to the sea-spirit as thanks. The sea-shaman, a quiet man with the salt-bleached hair of a lifetime on the cliffs, reads the tide's rise and fall in the pool, casts offerings of fish-blood and flowers into the water, and interprets the movements of the small creatures that live in its crevices — a crab, a limpet, a tiny eel — as messages from the deep. Voyages are blessed here, storms are read here, and the drowned are mourned here, their names carved into the soft limestone beside the pool. The air is cold, wet, and salt, and the sound of the sea is everywhere, a breathing rhythm that the shaman says is the voice of the spirit itself. At high tide the pool overflows and the shrine floods to the knee, and only the shaman stays, standing in the cold water, listening.
