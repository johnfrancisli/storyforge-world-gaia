The Temple of the Coiled Serpent is a deep-jungle temple-city being slowly reclaimed by the rainforest, built around a vast stepped pyramid of moss-covered limestone whose carvings of feathered serpents and goggled faces are still visible beneath the roots and vines that rive the stone. The city was abandoned centuries ago after a plague and a flood, and for generations it lay silent save for monkeys and jaguars — until a community of scholars, shamans, and displaced families returned to reclaim it. Now the great plaza before the pyramid is cleared and swept, the smaller temples and palaces around it are half-restored and half-ruined, and new thatched houses are built atop the old stone foundations, their wooden walls bright against the grey mossy ruins. Howler monkeys roar from the pyramid's summit at dawn and dusk, and flocks of oropendolas nest in the trees that shade the courtyards. The air smells of limestone dust, wet moss, burning copal from the restored shrines, and the sweet decay of the jungle pressing in on every side. It is a place of contradiction — living and dead, restored and ruined, sacred and profane — and the people who live here speak to the old spirits as neighbours, sometimes welcome, sometimes not.
