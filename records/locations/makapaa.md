Makapaa is the cliff-isle of the Tide Archipelago, a jagged tooth of wind-carved limestone and basalt that rises sheer from the deep water on every side, its eastern face a plunging cliff of grey rock streaked white with bird-droppings and draped in hanging ferns, its western face a slightly gentler series of ledges and shelves where the village clings like a barnacle to the rock. There is no beach — the waves break white against the base of the cliffs and the only way ashore is through a sea-cave at low tide or up a switchback path of hand-cut steps and rope-holds chiseled into the western face, so narrow that two people cannot pass. The houses are built on the ledges, their flat stone roofs weighted against the wind, their walls of stacked limestone and driftwood, and between them run narrow paths and rope-bridges over the gullies that channel rainwater down the cliff in the storms. The people of Makapaa are climbers and fishermen, lean and weathered, their hands rough as the rock they live on, and they lower themselves on rope-lines to the wave-cut shelves below to fish for the deep-water species that school around the island's base — monkfish, red snapper, and the great tunas that pass in the season. The air is always moving here, salt-wind and the cries of the sea-birds that nest in the cliff-cracks by the thousand, and the whole island smells of salt, guano, and the sweet heavy scent of fish smoking on the cliff-ledge racks.
