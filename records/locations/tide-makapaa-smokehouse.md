The Smokehouse Ledges are the fish-curing works of Makapaa, a series of natural shelves on the cliff's eastern face above the sea-cave, roofed with flat stone slabs and open to the wind, where the daily catch is gutted, split, and hung on racks of driftwood to dry and smoke over slow fires of driftwood and fragrant sandalwood scraped from the rare logs that wash up. The fires are kept low and smouldering, their smoke thick and sweet and brown, curling around the ranks of split fish — tuna, snapper, monkfish — that turn slowly in the wind, their flesh shrinking and darkening to a hard cured brown that will keep for months and is the island's chief trade-good, prized across the archipelago for journeys and the lean storm-season. The smell is overwhelming — wood-smoke, salt, and the rich oil of curing fish — and it hangs over the whole eastern cliff, marking Makapaa to any approaching canoe before the rock itself is visible. Women gut and split the fish at stone tables slick with blood and scales, the gulls screaming overhead and diving for offal thrown from the ledges, and a smoke-master tends the fires, judging the cure by smell and colour. At night the fires glow red through the smoke, and the cured fish hang like strange fruit in the dim light.
