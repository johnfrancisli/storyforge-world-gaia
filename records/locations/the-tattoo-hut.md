The Tattoo Hut is the ritual-house of Lani Atoll, a small dark hut of woven palm and hardwood set apart at the edge of the feast-ground, where the island's tattoo-master — a hereditary office held by a lineage said to have been taught the art by the sea-spirit itself — marks the bodies of the islanders with the spirals, waves, and sea-creature designs that record a person's lineage, deeds, and spirit-pacts, and that are the visible language of status and identity across the archipelago. The work is done with bone-comb mallets dipped in the ink of soot and candlenut-oil, tapped into the skin in long painful sessions, and the hut's air is thick with the smell of the oil, the green leaves chewed for pain, and the faint salt-metal scent of blood — a sacred smell, the tattoo-master says, for the marking is a sacrifice to the spirit that owns the design. The hut's walls are hung with the bone-combs of the lineage, each carved by its master, and the pattern-boards — painted bark-cloth showing the great designs — that a client may choose from or that the master may prescribe by right of lineage. A youth receives their first marking at coming-of-age, a voyager is marked for each great voyage, a chief for each Moot they preside over, and the master alone may add to or refuse a design, reading a person's right to a mark in the spirit-omens. The drum-beat of the mallet carries from the hut, and the islanders say a person is not truly of the sea until they have been marked.
