# The Song of the Great Serpent

The religion of [[location:verdania|Verdania]] has no name, because it does not need one. The Verdanians do not think of their relationship with the forest as a religion. It is simply the way things are. The forest is alive. The forest is aware. The forest is full of beings who are older than people and who deserve the same respect you would give any neighbor — more, because the forest can kill you and a neighbor probably will not. What the southern nations call religion, the Verdanians call life.

## Core Worldview

Everything is alive. Not metaphorically. The river has a self. The stone has a memory. The tree has a will. The Great Serpent — Coatl, the first being — flows through all of them like blood through a body. Coatl is not a god in the sense of a being who sits above the world and watches. Coatl is the world. The river is Coatl's vein. The canopy is Coatl's skin. The rain is Coatl's breath. To live in Verdania is to live inside a living being, and the living being knows you are there.

This means there is no separation between religion and daily life. There is no sacred and profane. Every act — felling a tree, crossing a river, planting a garden, building a house — is an interaction with a being, and interactions have protocols. You ask before you take. You give before you receive. You maintain the relationship, because the relationship is survival.

The shaman's role is not priestly. It is diplomatic. A shaman is the person who knows how to speak to the spirits, how to negotiate with them, and how to maintain the pacts that hold the community's place in the forest. A shaman who fails at diplomacy finds the river flooding the village, the game disappearing from the hunting grounds, or the path growing over in a single night. The forest does not punish. The forest responds, and the response is proportional to the offense.

## Deities and Spirits

The Great Serpent is the creator, the ancestor, and the living presence. Coatl is not worshipped — worship implies a distance that does not exist. Coatl is acknowledged, constantly, in every act and every word. A Verdanian does not say a prayer to Coatl. They sing, and the singing is both conversation and communion, because the Serpent hears through the roots and the water and the air.

Below Coatl are the great spirits — the Jaguar-Spirit, the River-Mother, the Sky-Bird, the Black-Earth. These are the beings who govern the major forces of the forest: the hunt, the water, the weather, the soil. They are not ranked. They are a community, and like any community, they have relationships, disagreements, and preferences. A shaman who works with the Jaguar-Spirit may find the River-Mother less cooperative, and the balancing of these relationships is the shaman's ongoing work.

Below the great spirits are the local spirits — every river, every stone, every ancient tree, every clearing has its own spirit with its own name. These are the spirits the people interact with daily. The spirit of the creek behind the village. The spirit of the old fig tree where the children play. The spirit of the rock that marks the path. They are small, specific, and as real as the people they live alongside. A Verdanian child learns the names of the local spirits the way a Valdrian child learns the names of the saints, but the spirits are not remote — they are here, in the creek, in the tree, in the rock, and they respond when spoken to.

See [[lore:spirit-hierarchy|the spirit hierarchy]] for how Verdania places the Great Serpent at the peak with the Jaguar-Spirit and river-spirits beneath.

## Sacred Practices

The central practice is song. The Verdanians sing to the forest, to the river, to the spirits, to the Great Serpent. The songs are not hymns in the structured sense. They are conversations — calls, responses, thanks, requests, apologies. A song sung before felling a tree is a request and an apology. A song sung at the river's edge is a greeting and an offering. A song sung at the healing canopy is a plea and a promise.

The songs are learned by ear, passed from teacher to student across generations. They are not written down. Writing them would fix them, and the spirits change, the rivers shift, the forest grows, and the songs must change with them. A song that was right for the old river may be wrong for the new course, and a shaman who sings the wrong song has not committed a ritual error — they have given offense, and the river will respond.

Offerings are constant. A piece of fruit left at the base of an old tree. A flower dropped into the river. A feather tied to a branch. The offerings are small, regular, and specific to the spirit receiving them. The Jaguar-Spirit receives meat. The River-Mother receives flowers. The Black-Earth receives seeds. The giving is the practice, and the regularity is the relationship.

The [[lore:festivals|Jaguar Moon]] and the Serpent-Waking are the great communal rites. The Jaguar Moon is the hunt-festival — a night of ritual hunting, feasting, and singing, honoring the Jaguar-Spirit and the contract between the people and the forest's game. The Serpent-Waking is the rain-festival — a rite performed at the start of the wet season, asking Coatl to bring the rains and warning the river spirits that the water will rise. Both are communal, joyful, and ancient.

## Moral Framework

The Verdanian moral code is not written. It is lived, and it is learned from the forest itself. The core principle is reciprocity: you take only what you give back, and you maintain the balance.

This means: never take more than you need. Never fell a tree without planting one. Never kill an animal without offering its spirit a song. Never cross a river without greeting it. Never enter a place without acknowledging its spirit. The rules are not abstract. They are practical, and the forest enforces them directly. A village that over-hunts finds the game gone. A village that pollutes its river finds the water undrinkable. The forest does not moralize. It responds, and the response is the teaching.

Same-gender pairings carry no penalty in Verdania. The spirits do not care about human pairings. The Great Serpent does not ask who a person loves. What matters is whether a person maintains their relationships — with the forest, with the spirits, with the community. A couple that tends their garden, honors their river-spirit, and contributes to the village is a good couple, regardless of gender. See [[lore:gender|the gender lore]] for the full detail.

Honesty is valued, but not in the southern sense. A Verdanian does not value blunt truth for its own sake. They value accurate perception — seeing things as they are, not as you wish them to be. A person who deceives themselves is more dangerous than a person who deceives others, because self-deception leads to bad relationships with the spirits, and bad relationships with the spirits are dangerous for everyone.

## Death and the Afterlife

The dead return to the forest. This is literal, not metaphorical. Verdanians are buried in the soil of the forest floor, often at the base of a significant tree, and the tradition holds that the dead become part of the tree — their spirit rooted in the wood, their body feeding the roots. The afterlife is not a separate place. It is here, in the forest, in the trees, in the soil. The dead are present in the canopy above and the earth below.

The ancestors are not remote. They are in the trees. A Verdanian who sits beneath the old fig where their grandmother is buried is sitting with their grandmother, and they may speak to her as they would to any ancestor. The response is felt in the movement of the leaves, the sound of the wind, the behavior of the birds. It is not a voice. It is a presence, and the presence is enough.

The [[location:ver-altacanopy-spiritnest|Spirit Nest]] is the most sacred burial site in Verdania — an ancient grove where the oldest trees hold the remains of generations. The spirits of the dead are said to be dense there, and the shamans go to the Nest to consult the ancestors on matters of great importance. A person who dies far from the forest — at sea, in another nation — is a person whose spirit must be guided home, and the shaman's most solemn duty is the guiding of lost dead back to the trees.

## Clergy and Authority

The shamans are the spiritual authority, but they are not clergy in the organized sense. A shaman is a person who has been chosen by the spirits — not elected, not ordained, but selected. The selection happens during initiation, and initiation is a journey.

A young person who shows the talent — the ability to hear the spirits, to sense the Great Serpent's movement, to see the patterns in the forest — is sent on a journey into the deep jungle. They go alone. They carry only a rattle and a song. They are gone for days or weeks, and what happens to them in the deep forest is between them and the spirits. Some do not return. Those who do return are shamans, and they carry the marks of their journey — a scar, a feather, a stone, a song no one has heard before.

The [[org:ver-hunter-clans|Clans of the Deep Canopy]] and the [[org:ver-shaman-circle|Circle of the Still Water]] are the organizational structures that connect shamans across communities. They are not churches. They are networks — ways for shamans to share knowledge, coordinate responses to forest-wide phenomena, and maintain the oldest pacts. A shaman who breaks a pact — who violates the agreement their community has with a river-spirit or a forest-spirit — may be stripped of recognition by the Circle, which means the other shamans will no longer work with them. In Verdania, this is close to exile.

## Relationship to Magic

Shamanic spirit-pacts are the religion. There is no separation. A shaman negotiates with the spirits of river, tree, stone, and beast, and the pact is a relationship — offerings, songs, respect, maintained over years and generations. The magic works because the relationship is real, and if the relationship decays, the magic decays with it.

The strongest shamans have pacts with ancient spirits that have been in their families for generations — a river-spirit that has known the shaman's grandmother and great-grandmother, that expects the same offerings and the same songs, that will withhold its power if the family neglects the tradition. This is not a spell. It is a friendship, and like all friendships, it requires maintenance.

## Tensions and Heresies

The main tension is between the forest peoples and the temple cities. The old temple cities — built before anyone can remember, half-reclaimed by the jungle, still humming with power — are sacred sites that no one fully controls. The shamans revere them. The communities fear them. And the temple-masons — the people who maintain what can be maintained of the old structures — have their own traditions, their own pacts, and their own authority, which sometimes conflicts with the shamans'. See [[thread:altacanopy-spirit-nest|the spirit nest thread]] for an active crisis.

The [[location:ver-templeruins-pyramidsummit|Serpent's Crown]] and the old temple complexes represent a version of Verdania's religion that is older than the current tribal structure. The temples imply a priesthood, a hierarchy, a written or carved tradition — things the current forest peoples do not have. The question of whether the temple tradition is the original faith and the current practice is a diminished descendant, or whether the current practice is the living faith and the temples are the fossilized remains, is a live argument that no one can settle, because the temples do not explain themselves.

The deepest tension is silence. The Great Serpent has gone quiet in places where it used to sing. The river-spirits in the eastern tributaries have stopped responding. The shamans do not know why, and the not-knowing is the worst thing that can happen to a people whose entire worldview is built on relationship. A spirit that is angry can be appeased. A spirit that is silent may be dead, and the death of a spirit is a death in the family — the biggest family there is.
