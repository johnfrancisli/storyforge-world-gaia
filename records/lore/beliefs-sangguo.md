# The Mandate of Heaven

The religion of [[location:sangguo|Sangguo]] has no gods in the way other nations understand them. There is no pantheon, no temple to a named deity, no priest who intercedes with a divine being. There is Tian — Heaven — which is not a god but an order. The structure of the universe itself. The way things are supposed to work. And there is the Mandate, which is the authority Heaven grants to those who rule justly and withdraws from those who do not.

## Core Worldview

Heaven is impersonal. It does not hear prayers. It does not answer pleas. It does not love, forgive, or punish in the way a god does. It simply is — the moral architecture of reality, as real and as indifferent as gravity. When a ruler governs with justice, the harvests are good, the rivers stay in their courses, and the people prosper. When a ruler governs with cruelty or negligence, the Mandate withdraws. Earthquakes come. Floods rise. The grain rots in the storehouse. These are not punishments from an angry god. They are the natural consequence of a world whose moral structure has been disrupted.

This means that politics in Sangguo is fundamentally religious. A warlord who claims the Mandate is claiming divine authority — not by divine right of birth, but by demonstrated competence. If the grain stores are full and the people are fed, the Mandate is evident. If the granaries are empty and the borders are raided, the Mandate has been withdrawn, and anyone strong enough to restore order may claim it in turn. This is the theological justification for civil war, and every warlord in Sangguo's history has used it.

Alongside the Mandate runs the older current of Daoist cosmology — the understanding that the universe is built on the balance of opposing forces, that the wise person does not fight the current but reads it and moves with it, and that chi — the vital energy that flows through all things — can be cultivated in the body as surely as it flows through the rivers and the earth. The two traditions are not separate. They are two faces of the same understanding: Heaven sets the order, and the wise person aligns with it.

## Deities and Spirits

There are no gods to worship. There are spirits to deal with.

The qilin, dragon-spirits, and ancestor-spirits are the three categories that matter. The qilin are celestial — they appear when the Mandate is strong and vanish when it is weak. A qilin sighting is the most powerful omen in Sangguo, and a warlord who can claim one has all the legitimacy they need. The dragon-spirits are earth-beings — the spirits of rivers, mountains, and weather. They are respected, sometimes propitiated, but not worshipped. They are forces of nature with personalities, and a wise magistrate takes them into account when planning a irrigation project.

The ancestor-spirits are the ones who receive daily attention. Every household maintains an ancestor altar — a tablet inscribed with the names of the lineage's dead, placed where the family can offer incense, rice wine, and the first portion of each meal. The ancestors are not remote. They are present, watchful, and invested in the continuation of the line. A family that prospers honors its ancestors. A family that fails has failed its ancestors first.

## Sacred Practices

The practices of Sangguo's religion are divided between the public and the domestic.

The public rites are performed by the scholar-ministries and the court ritualists. The most important is the Heavenly Sacrifice — a twice-yearly ceremony in which the ruler, or their designated representative, offers grain, silk, and jade to Heaven at the [[location:san-chudu-lotus-temple|Temple of the Lotus Ancestors]] or the state temple of whichever faction holds power. The rite is not prayer. It is acknowledgment — a formal recognition that the ruler's authority comes from Heaven and that the ruler accepts the responsibility it carries. A ruler who skips the Heavenly Sacrifice is making a statement, and the statement is usually "I do not fear the Mandate's withdrawal," which is a dangerous thing to say.

The domestic practices are the ancestor rites. Every household, from the warlord's palace to the peasant's hut, maintains the altar. The morning offering is incense and tea. The evening offering is a cup of rice wine. On the anniversaries of deaths and at the new year, the family gathers before the altar, and the eldest son leads the rite. The regularity is absolute. A missed offering is a failure of filial duty, and in Sangguo, there is no failure more serious.

Chi cultivation is both martial and spiritual. The [[location:san-jin-an-academy|Academy of the Five Classics]] and its sister institutions teach forms, meditation, and breath-work that are simultaneously combat training and religious discipline. A chi master who has cultivated for decades is not merely a formidable fighter — they are a person whose body is in alignment with Heaven's order, and that alignment is visible in their bearing, their health, and their power.

## Moral Framework

Filial piety is the highest virtue and the foundation of everything else. The duty to one's parents and ancestors is not one obligation among many. It is the obligation, from which all others derive. A person who is filial will be loyal, trustworthy, and just, because those qualities serve the family. A person who is not filial cannot be trusted in anything, because a person who fails their own blood will fail anyone.

This is the root of Sangguo's law against same-gender union. The legal code, derived from Confucian moral philosophy, treats the continuation of the family line as a cosmic duty. A same-gender pairing that produces no heirs breaks the chain of ancestor-obligation. It is not merely a personal choice — it is a failure of filial piety so severe that it undermines the Mandate itself, because the Mandate depends on the moral order of every household. The law is harsh: both parties may be sentenced to death. Enforcement is uneven — wealthy families buy silence, and the law falls hardest on those without protectors. But the theological logic is consistent. See [[lore:gender|the gender lore]] for the full legal detail.

The other virtues — benevolence (ren), righteousness (yi), ritual propriety (li), wisdom (zhi), and trustworthiness (xin) — are the pillars of the moral life. They are taught in the academies alongside strategy and calligraphy, and a scholar who masters them is considered qualified to govern. The assumption is that moral cultivation and administrative competence are the same skill. A person who understands virtue will govern well, and a person who governs well demonstrates virtue. This is not always true in practice, but it is the theory the entire system runs on.

## Death and the Afterlife

The dead become ancestor-spirits, but only if they are properly honored. A person who dies with descendants to tend their altar continues as an ancestor — present, watchful, part of the family. A person who dies without descendants becomes a wandering spirit, and wandering spirits are pitiable and dangerous in equal measure.

The afterlife is not a paradise or a punishment. It is a continuation — a shadow of the living world where the ancestors reside, receive offerings, and watch over the family. The quality of the afterlife depends on the quality of the offerings. An ancestor well-honored is content and powerful. An ancestor neglected is hungry and angry, and an angry ancestor-spirit can blight a family's fortune.

This creates a powerful incentive structure. A person who fails to produce heirs has not merely failed socially — they have condemned their ancestors to neglect and themselves to eventual wandering. The weight of this is enormous, and it is the deepest reason the lineage laws carry such force.

## Clergy and Authority

Sangguo does not have priests in the way other nations do. It has scholars.

The [[org:san-jade-brush-ministry|Jade Brush Ministry]] and the great academies serve as the religious authority, but their function is administrative and educational as much as spiritual. A scholar who has passed the examinations is qualified to perform the state rites, advise on matters of ritual propriety, and govern. The same person who interprets a passage of the classics may also draft agricultural policy and lead an army. The separation between religious and secular authority does not exist, because in Sangguo's understanding, they are the same thing.

The court ritualists are a specialized subset — scholars who have devoted themselves specifically to the rites, the omens, and the interpretation of Heaven's will. They read the stars, the harvests, the behavior of qilin, and the health of the rivers as signs of whether the Mandate is strong or weakening. A warlord who ignores the court ritualists does so at their peril.

## Relationship to Magic

Chi cultivation is the spiritual discipline of Sangguo, and it is simultaneously its martial tradition. There is no separation. The monk who meditates and the soldier who trains forms are practicing the same art at different levels of depth. The body is a microcosm of the universe — chi flows through it as it flows through the rivers and the earth — and to cultivate chi is to align the body with Heaven's order.

Spirit-pacts — agreements with qilin, dragon-spirits, and ancestor-spirits — are a separate practice, viewed with more ambivalence. The orthodox position is that spirit-pacts are acceptable when they serve the moral order and dangerous when they serve personal ambition. A warlord who consults a qilin for the good of the state is acting properly. A warlord who binds a dragon-spirit for personal power is on the path to losing the Mandate, because the Mandate belongs to those who serve Heaven, not to those who command spirits.

## Tensions and Heresies

The three-state truce is the overarching tension. Jin, Chu, and Wei each claim the Mandate, and each claims the others have lost it. The theological argument is existential — if the Mandate is singular, as orthodoxy insists, then only one state can hold it, and the others are illegitimate. If the Mandate can be divided, as some reformist scholars argue, then the truce has a theological basis. The reformists are not popular. The orthodox position is that the truce is a temporary expediency, not a permanent settlement, and the wars will resume because Heaven cannot be permanently partitioned.

A secondary tension concerns the academies. The [[org:great-academy|Great Academy of Jinan]] teaches the orthodox synthesis of Confucian ethics, Daoist cosmology, and chi cultivation. Rival academies in Chu and Wei teach variations that emphasize different elements — the Chu tradition leans toward Daoist mysticism and artistic cultivation, while the Wei tradition emphasizes legalist statecraft and military discipline. The three academies agree on the fundamentals but disagree on what the fundamentals are, which is the kind of argument that in Sangguo leads to war.

The deepest heresy is the suggestion that Heaven does not exist — that the Mandate is a political fiction with no cosmic backing. This view is held quietly by a few strategists who have seen too many brutal warlords prosper and too many just rulers fall. It is never spoken in public. The social and legal consequences of denying Heaven are nearly as severe as those for breaking the lineage laws, because if Heaven does not exist, then every claim to authority in Sangguo is merely force, and no one in power wants to say that aloud.
