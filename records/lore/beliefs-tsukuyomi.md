# The Way of the Eight Hundred Kami

The religion of [[location:tsukuyomi|Tsukuyomi]] is not a faith in the southern sense. There is no single god, no founding prophet, no sacred text handed down from a mountain. There are the kami — the spirits that live in everything — and there is the web of duty, purification, and ancestor-reverence that connects the living to them. The Tsukuyomi call it the Way of the Eight Hundred Kami, though the number is proverbial, not literal. There are far more than eight hundred.

## Core Worldview

Everything has a spirit. The waterfall has a spirit. The old cedar has a spirit. The village crossroads, the sword on the wall, the teapot that has been used for a hundred years — all have kami. The world is not divided into sacred and profane. The world is one fabric, and the kami are the threads. A person does not worship the kami so much as acknowledge them, maintain the relationship, and avoid giving offense.

The fundamental spiritual condition is impurity — kegare — which accumulates through contact with death, illness, blood, and certain transgressions. Impurity is not sin in the moral sense. It is more like spiritual dirt. It builds up, it spreads, and it can be washed away through purification rites. A person who carries kegare is not evil, but they are spiritually compromised, and the kami will not approach them. A shrine left unpurified weakens. A household that neglects its ancestors accumulates kegare. The solution is always the same: cleanse, renew, restore balance.

This is the ethos the Tsukuyomi speech reflects — restraint, balance, the avoidance of excess. Spiritual equilibrium is the goal. Not victory, not ecstasy, not salvation. Balance, maintained, day after day, through small correct actions.

## Deities and Spirits

The kami are ranked, though the ranking is less a hierarchy than a natural order, like the ranking of mountains. The great kami are bound at the [[location:tsu-tsukinomiyako-grand-shrine|Grand Shrine of Tsuki-no-Miyako]] — the celestial kami of sun, moon, and storm, whose names are spoken only in formal ritual. Below them are the land-kami: the spirits of rivers, forests, mountains, and villages. Below those are the household kami and the ancestor-spirits, the ones who live in the family shrine and receive daily offerings.

The yokai are a separate category. They are spirits too, but wild ones — the kitsune who run message shops, the tengu who teach swordsmanship in the mountains, the yuki-onna who appears in winter storms. The yokai are not worshipped. They are dealt with. The shrine network maintains the wards that keep the wilder yokai at a distance from settled areas, and a shrine keeper who neglects their rounds finds their ward weakening. The folk tradition of leaving offerings at crossroads and old trees for the minor yokai persists in the villages, tolerated by the institutional shrines but not officially endorsed.

See [[lore:spirit-hierarchy|the spirit hierarchy]] for how Tsukuyomi classifies its yokai from the low tsukumogami up through the kitsune and yuki-onna to the great kami.

## Sacred Practices

Purification is the daily practice. Every shrine performs the morning cleansing rite — water, salt, the correct words. Every household maintains the kamidana, the small shelf-shrine where incense is lit and rice and water are offered to the household kami and the ancestors. The offerings are small and regular: a cup of fresh water each morning, a bowl of rice at each meal, a branch of sakaki at the new year. The regularity matters more than the size.

The shrine calendar drives the communal rhythm. New Year purification, spring blossom-viewing, the summer festival where the portable shrine is carried through the streets, the autumn moon-viewing, the winter solstice cleansing. Each festival is both celebration and rite, and attendance is expected. A household that does not attend the festivals is noticed.

Pilgrimage is practiced but not required. The [[location:tsu-haru-shrine|Haru hilltop shrine]] and the Cedar Road pilgrimage route draw the devout, and a person who walks the full route is said to be cleaned of accumulated kegare. Most people make one significant pilgrimage in their lifetime. Some make several.

## Moral Framework

Duty is the highest virtue. Duty to family, to clan, to the shrine, to the social order. The Tsukuyomi do not have a concept of individual sin in the way the Valdrian Dawnfire does. They have a concept of failure — the failure to meet an obligation, to maintain a relationship, to perform a rite correctly. Failure is not eternal. It is repaired through correction, purification, and the restoration of balance.

The weightiest obligation is to one's ancestors. The family shrine is the center of the household, and the care of it is non-negotiable. A person who neglects the ancestor shrine is not merely careless — they are allowing the family's spiritual connection to decay. This extends to the lineage itself: producing heirs to carry the shrine forward is a duty, not a preference. This is the root of Tsukuyomi's disapproval of same-gender pairings — not law, but the quiet conviction that a person who does not fulfill their household duty has disrupted the kami-order. See [[lore:gender|the gender lore]] for the full social detail.

Restraint is valued over expression. A person who speaks too much, displays too much, or seeks too much is seen as spiritually unbalanced. The ideal is the person who does what is correct without announcing it, whose devotion is visible in the care of their shrine and the precision of their conduct, not in the volume of their prayers.

## Death and the Afterlife

Death is the primary source of kegare, and the handling of the dead is the most carefully regulated religious practice in Tsukuyomi. A death in the household requires immediate purification — the family, the room, the objects the deceased touched. The funeral rite is performed by a shrine priest, and the period of formal impurity lasts forty days, during which the family avoids shrine visits and communal festivals.

The dead become ancestor-spirits. They are not distant — they reside in the household shrine and receive daily offerings. They are consulted, in a sense: a family member may sit before the kamidana and speak to the ancestors, and the response is felt as a sense of rightness or wrongness, not as a voice. The ancestors are believed to watch over the family, guide its decisions, and contribute to its spiritual well-being, provided they are properly honored.

A spirit that is not honored — that has no descendants to tend its shrine — becomes restless. These are the ghosts that the shrine network wards against, the unquiet dead who linger at crossroads and abandoned houses. The Tsukuyomi do not fear their ancestors. They fear the ancestors no one cares for.

## Clergy and Authority

The kannushi (priests) and miko (shrine priestesses) are the clergy of Tsukuyomi. They are trained at the Grand Shrine and at the major regional shrines, and their authority is both spiritual and practical. A kannushi performs rites, maintains wards, interprets omens, and serves as the community's link to the kami. A miko channels spirit-energy through ceremony and dance, and the most skilled among them can sense kegare and spiritual disturbance directly.

Training is long. A shrine apprentice spends years learning the rituals, the calendar, the correct words and gestures for each rite. The precision is not aesthetic — it is functional. A rite performed incorrectly does not work, and a shrine keeper who habitually errs will find their wards failing and their community vulnerable.

The [[org:tsu-shrine-network|Moon Shrine Network]] is the institutional backbone. It coordinates the shrine calendar, assigns priests to shrines, and mediates disputes between shrines and clans. The Network holds real political power — a clan that falls out of favor with the Network finds its local shrine poorly staffed and its wards weakening, and in Tsukuyomi, that is a serious vulnerability.

## Relationship to Magic

Shrine magic — the ritual channeling of spirit-energy through ceremony, ofuda talismans, and purification rites — is the practice of Tsukuyomi's religion. There is no separation. The miko who channels spirit-energy is performing a religious act. The kannushi who carves an ofuda is creating a sacred object. The magic works because the relationship with the kami is maintained, and if the relationship decays, the magic decays with it.

This means that magic in Tsukuyomi is not a talent. It is a discipline. Power scales with devotion and precision, not with innate ability. A shrine keeper who neglects their rounds finds their power fading. A miko who maintains her practice for decades grows in capability. The system rewards constancy, not brilliance.

## Tensions and Heresies

The main tension is between the institutional shrines and the folk tradition. The Grand Shrine and the Moon Shrine Network represent the orthodox practice — precise, formal, politically integrated with the shogunate and the clans. The folk tradition is older: the village practices of leaving offerings at crossroads, appeasing minor yokai, consulting the old women who know the local spirits but hold no shrine rank. The orthodoxy tolerates the folk tradition because it cannot easily suppress it, and because the folk practices do work, after a fashion. But a kannushi who incorporates folk elements into formal rites risks censure.

A deeper tension concerns the yokai. The institutional position is that yokai are wild spirits to be warded against, not worshipped. But some communities — particularly those near the mountains, where tengu training grounds and kitsune message shops are part of daily life — have developed relationships with specific yokai that look very much like worship. The shrines look the other way when it suits them and crack down when it does not.

The most sensitive tension is the succession. The shogun is elderly, and the Ashikara clan's spiritual authority is tied to the Grand Shrine's support. If the succession is contested, the shrine network may be forced to choose a side, and a split in the shrines would weaken the wards across the entire nation. No one says this aloud. Everyone knows it.
