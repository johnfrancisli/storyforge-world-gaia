# Geopolitics of Gaia

Seven nations share one continent and the seas around it. They are not allies and they are not enemies. They are neighbors, and neighbors spend most of their time minding their own business and some of their time minding everyone else's.

---

## The Continental Map

Gaia is a single landmass with a long north-south spine, fringed by mountains and wrapped in sea. The arrangement is uneven. The north is cold and hard. The south is hot and older. The east breaks into islands. The west holds the most farmland and the oldest roads.

Hrafnland sits at the top of the continent -- boreal fjords, glacial horns, black water and grey rock. The land is narrow and vertical, cut by inlets that bite deep into the coast. South of Hrafnland, across a strait choked with skerries, the land broadens into Valdris: temperate valleys, rolling hills, and the kind of green that comes from rain. Valdris stretches west-central, the most connected nation on the continent, with roads that still mostly work and bridges that have not yet fallen.

South of Valdris, across the strip of churned earth called No Man's Land, the land thickens into Verdania. The rainforest begins at the border and does not stop -- a canopy so dense that the ground beneath it stays wet and dark. Verdania fills the south-central continent, and its coastline faces east, across the sea, toward Tsukuyomi.

Sangguo lies to the southwest, a land of river basins and terraced mountains, where the water comes down out of the highlands and spreads into paddies and canals. Its western reaches climb into red rock canyons, and through those canyons runs the Silk Pass -- the only overland route south to Al-Khayzar. Al-Khayzar sits at the bottom of the continent, where the rivers dry up and the land opens into arid desert, broken by oases that hold the only water for days in any direction.

Tsukuyomi is not on the continent at all. It lies to the east, an archipelago of volcanic islands across the sea from both Verdania and Sangguo. The water between is crossed by trade ships and sometimes by pirates, and the crossing is long enough that the islands feel like a separate world even though they are not.

The Tide Archipelago is farther still -- far east, out in the open ocean beyond Tsukuyomi, warm-water islands scattered across water so wide that a voyager can sail for days and see nothing but sea and sky. It is the most isolated nation in Gaia, and the people who live there prefer it that way.

Travel between nations is slow. The conventional routes are sea lanes and mountain passes, and a journey from one nation to the next by ordinary means is a month or more depending on the route and the season. The King's Road in Valdris and the river networks of Sangguo are the best-maintained. The sea routes are reliable in good weather and deadly in bad. The Silk Pass is passable year-round but brutal in summer heat. The northern straits freeze in winter.

Then there are the Old Roads. The spirit-paths that once connected every nation -- most are broken, overgrown, or sealed. A few stretches still work. Finding one is rare and valuable, and walking a working stretch turns a month of travel into an afternoon. The entrances are hidden, and the people who live near the working stretches often keep them shut, because the Roads do not check who is using them. The largest known fragments are in Al-Khayzar near Khalesh, in the deep jungle of Verdania near the old temple cities, and in the mountains between Sangguo and Valdris where the stone still humms faintly if you press your ear to it. No one has found a working stretch that connects to Tsukuyomi or the Tide Archipelago. The Roads do not cross water.

## The Borderlands

Borders in Gaia are not clean lines on a map. They are zones -- places where two nations press against each other and the ground bears the marks. Some are contested with arms. Some are contested with tolls. One has been quiet so long that both sides have forgotten what the argument was about, which does not mean it is over.

### The Verdmarch (Valdris-Verdania Border)

The ground between Valdris's southern hills and Verdania's northern canopy is a strip of churned earth about twenty miles wide. The stone walls built across it are broken now -- knocked down, rebuilt, knocked down again. The trenches still hold water. The mud is grey and deep enough to take a boot off if you are not careful.

Soldiers on both sides call it No Man's Land. The Valdrian Verdmarch Constabulary holds forward keeps here -- squat stone towers with iron-bound gates, manned in rotation, supplied by cart from the southern garrisons. The keeps are cold and damp and the constables count the days until their rotation ends.

The Verdanian Wardens of the Briar do not build in stone. They work in thorn -- barricades of woven bramble, deadfalls of weighted timber, pit-traps covered with leaf litter. The forest does their work for them. Where the Verdmarch borders the canopy, the trees send roots into the broken walls and pull them apart over seasons. The Wardens expand the thorn line slowly, and the Constabulary burns it back slowly, and neither side has gained meaningful ground in a generation.

The land smells of wet earth and old iron. Rain collects in the craters. Foxes den in the collapsed trenches. Sometimes a deer walks across No Man's Land at dawn, and both sides watch it go, and no one fires.

### The Silk Pass (Sangguo-Al-Khayzar Border)

The Silk Pass is a mountain route through red rock canyons, the only overland trade route between Sangguo's western riverlands and Al-Khayzar's northern desert. The pass is narrow -- two carts cannot pass abreast in the tightest stretches -- and hot, the canyon walls radiating the day's heat long after the sun has dropped behind the ridgeline.

Toll stations sit at both ends. The Sangguo station is a stone gatehouse with a weighbridge and a clerk who records every load. The Al-Khayzar station is a domed customs house with a fountain in the courtyard, which the caravanners appreciate more than the toll itself. Caravans move spice north and silk south, and the pass sees traffic every day the weather allows.

The pass is contested economically, not militarily. Both nations profit from the trade, and both know it. The argument is about the tolls -- who takes more, who sets the rate, who gets to tax the caravans that pass through the narrow middle where neither nation's claim is entirely clear. The argument has been going on for decades and is conducted in letters between trade ministers, not in blood.

Bandits are the real problem. They hide in the canyon side-gorges, the narrow clefts that branch off the main route where a horse cannot follow. They hit caravans in the stretches between the toll stations, where neither garrison can respond in time. The caravans hire guards. The guards cost more than the tolls. Everyone does the math and moves on.

### The Basalt Skerries (Valdris-Hrafnland Border)

The strait between Hrafnland's southern coast and Valdris's northern shore is choked with a chain of jagged sea-rocks and broken islands called the Basalt Skerries. The rocks are dark, ice-slicked, and sharp -- basalt that the glaciers broke and the sea has not yet worn smooth. The channels between them are navigable only by shallow-draft vessels, which suits Hrafnland's longships and does not suit Valdris's heavier patrol galleys.

In winter the rocks freeze. Spray coats the stone in black ice, and the channels close to anything that cannot be dragged out of the water and carried. Men have died stepping from a boat to a rock they could not see was frozen. The passage becomes deadly, and the skerries go quiet except for the wind.

Both nations claim fishing rights in the skerries, and the fishing is good -- the rocks shelter cod and halibut in their lee. The claim is theoretical in winter and practical in summer, when fishing boats from both sides work the same water and occasionally anchor in the same coves. It is not a friendly arrangement, but it has not come to blows.

The real trouble is the raiders. Hrafnland raiders sometimes use the skerries as a hidden route south, threading the shallow channels in longships that a Valdrian galley cannot follow. Valdris's Northwatch garrison is meant to prevent this -- a chain of watch-posts on the northern shore, manned year-round, watching the rocks for movement. They see most of what comes through. Not all.

### The Moon Strait (Tsukuyomi-Verdania Sea Route)

The water between Tsukuyomi's western shores and Verdania's eastern coast is called the Moon Strait, though the name is older than anyone's explanation for it. It is open water, not a channel -- wide enough that you lose sight of land on the second day if the weather is clear, sooner if it is not.

Trade ships cross the strait regularly. Verdania sends featherwork, river-pearls, and fermented floral wine east. Tsukuyomi sends lacquerware, shrine-craft, and folded steel west. The ships are sturdy, broad-beamed, and crewed by sailors who know the route well enough to sleep through the nights.

The crossing takes a week in good weather. In bad weather it takes two, and some ships do not arrive at all. The strait is not the worst water in Gaia, but it does not forgive carelessness.

Pirates work the route. They operate out of uninhabited volcanic islets scattered along the crossing -- bare rock with a hidden cove and a crew that knows the approach. They take cargo and leave the crews alive, mostly, because a dead sailor cannot spread the word that paying the toll is cheaper than fighting. The tolls are not small. The trade ships factor them in.

### The Long Passage (Sangguo-Tsukuyomi Sea Route)

The sea route between Sangguo's eastern ports and Tsukuyomi's southern harbors is longer and rougher than the Moon Strait. It carries more volume. Sangguo silk and grain flow east in bulk, and Tsukuyomi silver and shrine-craft flow west. The ships are larger, the crews are bigger, and the crossings run on schedules that the merchant houses set by the season.

The passage takes two weeks in good weather, three in bad. Storms in the Long Passage are sudden and fierce -- the kind that build in an afternoon and hit before a crew can shorten sail. The sailors who work the route say the sea is flat and calm right up until it is not, and the trick is to read the sky before the sky decides to read you.

Pirates are less common here than on the Moon Strait -- the open water is too wide and the ships too large to take easily. But the storms do work that pirates cannot, and a ship lost to weather is a ship lost to everyone. Insurance is a word the merchant houses use often and quietly.

## Linguistic Texture

The nations of Gaia do not share a language, though most traders speak enough of two or three to get by. What differs is not only the words but the weight behind them -- what a culture counts as important enough to have its own word, and what it leaves to context.

### Valdris

Valdris speech is heavy and formal, built on Latinate and Norman-feudal roots. Sentences are constructed like walls -- load-bearing, balanced, meant to last. A Valdrian does not say things quickly. They say them correctly, and the correctness is the point.

The vocabulary reflects what the culture values. A **verdmarch** is a border territory held under martial law -- a word that contains the assumption that borders are military problems. The **haut-cour** is the high judiciary, and the term carries the weight of centuries of precedent. **Ferrobind** is tempered structural ironwork, and a Valdrian mason says the word with the same respect a Tsukuyomi priest gives a shrine name.

Social worth in Valdris is measured by land title, guild standing, or service to the crown. A person is introduced by what they hold and whom they serve. The speech reflects it -- a Valdrian greeting asks where you are from and what your house is, in that order, and the answers place you in a hierarchy that everyone in the room understands without being told.

### Verdania

Verdanian speech is melodic, built on Provençal and Italian roots with an elven vernacular woven through it like thread through cloth. The cadence rises and falls. Sentences end on open vowels. It is a language meant to be sung, and sometimes it is -- the Verdanians will break into song mid-conversation without finding it strange.

The words for living things are specific. **Verdemiel** is first-pressed floral wine, and the term means exactly that -- the first pressing, from the first flowers of the season, nothing else. **Radicanto** is tree-song, the living root path that shamans walk to speak with the forest, and the word cannot be translated without losing the part where the roots are singing. **Solombra** is the boundary where sunlight fails beneath the ancient canopy -- the line between the green light above and the dark below, and Verdanians use the word to mean the edge of any known thing.

Time in Verdania is measured in vintages and seasons, not years. A Verdanian does not say "three years ago." They say "three vintages past," or "three dry seasons back." The land sets the calendar, and the calendar is not regular, because the land is not regular.

### Tsukuyomi

Tsukuyomi speech is understated and poetic, built on contextual nuance and layers of honorific that shift with rank, relationship, and circumstance. What is not said carries as much weight as what is. A Tsukuyomi speaker can decline an offer, express displeasure, and convey a warning in the same sentence, and a listener who is not trained in the register will hear only a polite refusal.

The vocabulary is precise about things other languages leave vague. A **kage-bito** is a shadow-agent -- not a spy, not an assassin, but a specific role in the clan system that carries obligations and permissions that both parties understand. **Shinryoku** is spirit-resonance, the quality of a place or object that holds spiritual weight, and a shrine keeper uses the word the way a Valdrian mason uses *ferrobind* -- as a term for something structural and real. **Tsukiyo-chikai** is an oath made under the open moon, and it binds differently than an oath made in daylight, because the moon sees and the sun does not, and that matters.

The ethos is restraint. A Tsukuyomi speaker says less than they mean and expects the listener to find the rest. Spiritual equilibrium is the goal -- not victory, not dominance, but balance maintained. Ancestral debt is real and counted. A clan does not forget what it owes, and it does not forget what it is owed.

### Sangguo

Sangguo speech is measured and dense, built on four-character idioms and dialectical proverbs that compress entire arguments into a phrase. A Sangguo scholar can end a debate with a single idiom, and everyone in the room knows who won and why. The language rewards study -- the more proverbs you know, the more precisely you can speak, and precision is power.

The vocabulary is built for strategy and statecraft. **Tie-shan** is iron mountain -- stubborn defense, the kind that does not break because it was built not to. **Shui-dao** is water-path -- trade opportunism, the principle of flowing where the opening is, and the term is used as both compliment and accusation. **He-xin** is harmonious agreement, and it is the most loaded term in the language, because a Sangguo speaker uses it to mean an agreement that is harmonious on the surface and tense beneath, and everyone who hears it knows which kind they mean.

Power in Sangguo runs through bureaucracy. The language reflects it -- there are seventeen words for different kinds of official documents, and a magistrate who uses the wrong one in a report will be corrected by a superior who noticed. Speech is formal in court and direct in private, and the distance between the two registers is where most of the actual politics happens.

### Hrafnland

Hrafnland speech is guttural and rhythmic, built on Old Norse roots and the kennings -- compound metaphors that say a thing by naming it as something else. A ship is a wave-stallion. The sea is the whale-road. A sword is a wound-seed. The kennings are not decoration. They are how the language works, and a Hrafnlander who speaks without them sounds flat and strange.

The vocabulary is physical and unadorned. **Is-brand** is frostbite, or a weapon made of winter -- the word means both, and context separates them. **Skjold-lag** is shield-brotherhood, the bond between warriors who have stood in the same shield wall, and it is a stronger word than family in Hrafnland. **Ravn-ting** is the clan council gathered under ravens, and it means exactly that -- the jarls meet outdoors, under the open sky, and the ravens that circle above are taken as witnesses.

Candor is the rule. A Hrafnlander says what they mean and does not soften it. Politeness in the Valdrian sense reads as dishonesty -- if you are angry, say so, and if you are not, do not perform it. Physical fortitude is respected and weakness is named, but weakness is not contemptible. It is simply a fact, and facts are for saying aloud.

### Al-Khayzar

Al-Khayzar speech is eloquent and ornate, built on Arabic and Persian poetic traditions where the beauty of the expression and the accuracy of the content are not separate concerns. A well-spoken sentence in Al-Khayzar is one that is both true and shaped well, and a true sentence said badly is considered less than it should be.

The vocabulary treats knowledge and water as sacred. A **qanat-dar** is a keeper of the deep water channels -- the underground aqueducts that carry water from the highlands to the desert cities -- and the title carries religious weight. A qanat-dar who fails their channels has failed something larger than a job. **Zill-nur** is shadow-light, the celestial alignment of sun and shade that marks specific times of day for prayer and navigation, and the term is used for any moment when two opposites meet and something becomes visible. **Souk-aqil** is a market arbitrator, the figure who settles disputes in the bazaar with authority that is commercial, legal, and moral at once.

Speech in Al-Khayzar is negotiation. Even casual conversation has a current of exchange -- information offered, information expected, the relationship between speakers measured in what each is willing to give. A silence in Al-Khayzar is not empty. It is a space where both sides are calculating.

### Tide Archipelago

Tide Archipelago speech is warm and oral, song-like in cadence, built on metaphor and the imagery of navigation. The sea is the source of nearly every figure of speech, because the sea is the world these people know, and the land is something you visit.

**Hoku-pae** is star-path -- one's chosen course in life, named for the star paths that voyagers follow across open water. To ask someone their hoku-pae is to ask what they are doing with their life, and it is a serious question, not small talk. **Moana-anga** is the deep ocean as a metaphor for the unknown -- the water beyond the reef, the place where the stars are the only map, and the term is used for anything vast and uncharted, including a person's grief. **Tuitui** is to pass a message by song, and it is not metaphor. The wayfinder songs carry words between islands across water that no boat has crossed, and the message arrives because someone sang it and someone else heard.

Story and song carry law, history, and emotion in the Archipelago. There is no written constitution. There is no archive of scrolls. There are the songs, and the songs are kept by the people who learned them from the people who learned them, and the chain goes back further than anyone can count. A dispute settled in song stays settled, because the song remembers the settlement, and the song outlasts the people who made it.

## Terrains of Gaia

The land fights differently in each nation. The ground beneath your feet, the sightlines ahead, the way water and stone and cold shape what is possible -- these are not abstractions. They are the things that decide who walks away from a fight and who does not.

### Valdris

The Valdrian landscape is built for defense -- trenches, broken stone walls, moderate hills, and palisades of sharpened timber. The hills give elevation, and elevation gives the advantage of the high ground, which in Valdris means you see the enemy first and they climb to reach you. The old trench lines from the last war are still here, half-collapsed, holding rainwater and rust. The new ones are dug deeper.

The ground is grey mud under leaden skies. It rained yesterday. It will rain tomorrow. The mud slows movement, fouls equipment, and turns a charge into a slog. A Valdrian commander thinks about the mud the way a Tsukuyomi commander thinks about mist -- it is a constant, and you plan around it or you do not plan well.

### Verdania

The Verdanian forest is the terrain, and the forest is against you if you are not of it. Dense canopy blocks the sky. Undergrowth -- fern, vine, root, fallen limb -- blocks movement. The ground is marsh and mud, soft underfoot, pooling with water that does not drain because the roots hold it. Sightlines are short. A person ten yards ahead is a shape, not a face.

The light comes through in dapples -- green, shifting, broken by mossy branches. It is beautiful and it is difficult. Foliage conceals movement and conceals threat equally. The Wardens of the Briar know the forest the way a Valdrian mason knows stone -- by feel, by sound, by the way the ground compresses under weight. An outsider in the Verdanian forest is loud, slow, and visible, and they do not know it until the forest tells them.

### Tsukuyomi

Tsukuyomi terrain is layered and precise -- bamboo thickets that rattle when you move through them, riven stone bridges over cold streams, mist basins where the ground breathes fog, and steep cliffsides where the path is cut into the rock and a wrong step is a long fall.

The mist is the constant. It rises from the water and pools in the low ground, and it does not burn off until midday, and by evening it is back. Sightlines that were clear at noon are gone by dusk. The ground is quiet -- packed earth and stone that do not echo under a soft footfall -- and silence rewards the patient. A fight in Tsukuyomi is often decided by who heard the other first.

### Sangguo

Sangguo terrain is shaped by water and embankment -- canal sluices that can be opened or closed to flood a field, terraced embankments that hold the rice paddies in steps up a hillside, and walled courtyards that turn a village into a series of choke points. The land has been engineered for agriculture, and that engineering is also a defense.

Water shapes every engagement. A paddy is knee-deep mud under a sheet of water -- slow to cross, loud to move through, and it offers no cover. An embankment is a wall of packed earth that holds the high ground along a canal. Red lacquered pavilions stand above the river mist on the terraces, and they are landmarks and rally points and, in a fight, the thing both sides want to hold.

### Hrafnland

Hrafnland terrain is frozen and unforgiving -- permafrost glades where the ground cracks underfoot, basalt boulder fields that break any formation that tries to cross them, sleet trenches that fill with ice water, and black ice crevasses that open in the ground without warning. The cold is not weather. It is terrain.

The frozen earth punishes the reckless. A forced march in Hrafnland costs toes as well as time. The granite sea cliffs rise over grey water, and the wind off the strait cuts through cloth and leather and finds the skin beneath. Combat here is slow, close, and costly, and the side that manages its warmth and its footing wins more often than the side with the larger numbers.

### Al-Khayzar

Al-Khayzar terrain is open and draining -- shifting sand dunes that swallow footsteps and make every step cost twice what it should, sun-baked adobe ruins that offer cover and shadow and nothing else, oasis marsh where the only green grows thick around the only water, and subterranean cistern floors where the fighting gets close and dark.

The deep sand drains stamina. A run across dunes is a walk within a hundred yards, and a walk becomes a stagger soon after. The open flats offer no cover -- a person on the sand is visible for miles, and the sun is a weight on the back of the neck. Amber dunes roll beneath an azure sky, and the land is beautiful and empty and it will kill someone who does not respect it.

### Tide Archipelago

In the Tide Archipelago, the ocean is the terrain. Coral reefs hide beneath the surface and break hulls. Sandy shallows let a canoe through and stop anything deeper. Volcanic rock shelves cut feet and grip wrong. Open water is the field, and the field moves.

Reefs hide and expose with the tide. Currents push and pull, and a fighter who knows the current uses it and one who does not fights the water as well as the enemy. A wrong step on volcanic rock means a slashed foot, and a slashed foot in salt water means infection, and infection in the Archipelago means the nearest healer is a sail away. The terrain here is alive, and it does not take sides.
